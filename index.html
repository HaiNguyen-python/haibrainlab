Lesson 1 · What is the brain made of?

Your brain weighs about 1.4 kg, the size of two fists, and uses about 20 watts, like a dim light bulb. It is made of about 86 billion neurons (nerve cells) plus roughly the same number of helper cells called glia, which feed, insulate and clean the neurons.

A neuron is a cell shaped like a tree. It has three parts:

  • Dendrites are the branches. They listen to thousands of other neurons.
  • The cell body adds up what the dendrites hear and decides whether to fire.
  • The axon is the cable that carries the decision, sometimes a metre long (from the lower spinal cord to the toe). It ends in terminals that touch the next neurons.

Neurons never actually touch. The tiny gap where a terminal meets the next cell is a synapse. You have around 100 trillion of them. Lesson 3 is about them.

Lesson 2 · How a neuron "fires"

A neuron is like a tiny battery. It pumps salt ions so that the inside is slightly negative compared to the outside (about −65 millivolts, a twentieth of an AA battery). This is its resting potential.

When enough excitement arrives at the dendrites, the voltage creeps up. If it crosses a threshold (about −50 mV), tiny doors in the membrane called sodium channels fly open. Sodium rushes in, the voltage flips positive for one millisecond, then potassium channels open and reset it. That one-millisecond pulse is a spike, also called an action potential. It travels down the axon like a wave in a stadium.

Three rules that explain almost everything:
  • All or none. A spike is always the same size. A neuron cannot send a "bigger" spike, only more spikes per second. Strong input = fast firing.
  • Threshold. Small inputs do nothing. They must add up. That is why attention matters: it turns up the volume on inputs you care about.
  • Refractory pause. After a spike the neuron needs ~2 ms before it can fire again, so the maximum is a few hundred spikes per second.

In the app: the Neuron tab lets you inject current into a simulated neuron and watch the spike form. "Hodgkin–Huxley" is the 1952 model that won a Nobel Prize for explaining exactly this. The sliders gNa and gK are the sodium and potassium doors; set gNa to zero and the neuron cannot fire, which is what the poison in puffer fish does.

Lesson 3 · Synapses: how cells talk, and how they learn

When a spike reaches a terminal, it makes little bubbles (vesicles) release a chemical, a neurotransmitter, into the 20-nanometre gap. The chemical lands on receptors on the next cell's dendrite and nudges its voltage. Then it is cleaned up and the synapse is ready again, all within a few milliseconds.

Two kinds of synapse: excitatory ones push the next cell toward firing (main chemical: glutamate); inhibitory ones push it away (main chemical: GABA). About four in five synapses are excitatory, but the inhibitory fifth decides what gets through, like brakes on a car.

This is where learning lives. A synapse has a strength: how big a nudge it gives. When the sending cell fires just before the receiving cell, the synapse grows stronger (more receptors, a bigger spine). This is called long-term potentiation (LTP), and the timing rule is called STDP. "Cells that fire together wire together." A memory is not a file; it is a pattern of strengthened synapses.

Lesson 4 · Many neurons together: networks and brain waves

One neuron is simple. The magic is in the crowd. When thousands of neurons excite and inhibit each other, they start to fire in rhythm, like an audience clapping in time. These rhythms are what an EEG (electrodes on the scalp) picks up as brain waves.

WaveSpeedYou are…
Delta0.5–4 per secondin deep sleep (memories are being filed)
Theta4–8drowsy, dreaming, or your hippocampus is storing/recalling

Gamma30+intensely focused, binding details into one perception

The balance between excitation and inhibition is everything. Too little brake and the whole network fires at once: that is an epileptic seizure. Too little gas and it goes quiet: that is anaesthesia or deep sleep.

Lesson 5 · The map: which part does what

The wrinkled outer layer is the cortex, only 2–4 mm thick but folded so much that unfolded it would cover a pillowcase. The folds are called gyri (ridges) and sulci (grooves). The cortex is divided into four lobes per side. Underneath sit older, faster structures that handle emotion, memory and survival.

Lesson 6 · How learning and memory actually work

Learning is not "putting information in". It is changing synapses, and it happens in stages:

1. Attentionchemicals (acetylcholine, noradrenaline) turn up the inputs you focus on. No attention, no learning.
2. Encodingthe hippocampus binds the pieces of the moment into one pattern and starts strengthening synapses (LTP).
3. Taggingif it mattered (surprise, reward, emotion), dopamine or noradrenaline marks the pattern as worth keeping.
4. Consolidationduring sleep the hippocampus replays the pattern to the cortex, where it is stored long term.
5. Retrievalrecalling rebuilds the pattern, makes it stronger, and can change it a little each time.

Kinds of memory

  • Working memory: the 3–5 things you hold in mind right now (prefrontal cortex). Lasts seconds. Easily overloaded.
  • Episodic memory: what happened to you (hippocampus + cortex).
  • Semantic memory: facts and words (cortex, mainly temporal lobe).
  • Procedural memory: skills like cycling or typing (basal ganglia + cerebellum). Survives even when the hippocampus is damaged.

What this means for studying (each tip has a reason above)

  • One thing at a time. Attention is the gate, and it does not split well.
  • Make it meaningful. Connect new facts to things you know; more connections, more retrieval routes.
  • Test yourself instead of re-reading. Retrieval strengthens the synapses; re-reading only feels like learning.
  • Space it out. Three short sessions over a week beat one long one, because each recall triggers another round of strengthening.
  • Sleep. Consolidation happens then. An all-nighter deletes the night's filing.
  • Move. Exercise releases BDNF, a growth factor that helps the hippocampus form new connections.
  • Manage stress. Short stress sharpens; weeks of cortisol block LTP and shrink the hippocampus.

Lesson 7 · Feelings are brain states with a body attached

Emotions are not "in the heart"; they are fast programs that prepare the body. The amygdala detects something important (a threat, a face, a chance) within milliseconds. It tells the hypothalamus, which tells the body through hormones and the autonomic nervous system: heart rate, breathing, sweating, digestion. The cortex then notices those body changes and the thoughts around them, and we call the whole package a feeling.

  • Fear = amygdala + adrenaline + noradrenaline. Fast road (thalamus → amygdala) fires before you know why.
  • Joy / reward = dopamine (wanting, "do it again") + endorphins and serotonin (liking, the warm glow).
  • Stress = cortisol. Fine for an hour, corrosive for weeks.
  • Love / bonding = oxytocin + dopamine, and the amygdala going quiet.
  • Calm = GABA and serotonin tone, prefrontal cortex holding the amygdala down.

The prefrontal cortex can talk the amygdala down. That is what slow breathing, naming the feeling, reappraisal ("this is a challenge, not a threat") and therapy train. It is a skill, and like all skills it is synapses getting stronger.

Lesson 8 · Your senses: from light and sound to experience

Seeing. Light hits the retina at the back of the eye, where 120 million rods (dim light) and 6 million cones (colour) turn it into spikes. The optic nerve carries them to the thalamus and on to the visual cortex (V1) at the very back of the head. From there two streams split: a "what" stream down into the temporal lobe (objects, faces, words) and a "where/how" stream up into the parietal lobe (position, movement, reaching). About a third of your cortex works on vision.

Hearing. The eardrum and three tiny bones push on the cochlea, a snail-shaped tube where hair cells are tuned like piano keys, high notes at one end and low at the other. That order (tonotopy) is kept all the way to the auditory cortex. You locate a sound by comparing the arrival time at the two ears, down to a few millionths of a second.

Smell is the odd one out: it skips the thalamus and goes almost directly to the amygdala and hippocampus, which is why a smell can bring back a childhood memory with its full emotion. Touch from the whole body is mapped on the somatosensory cortex, with huge areas for lips and fingertips and tiny ones for the back.

Perception is prediction. The brain does not passively receive; it guesses what is out there and corrects the guess with the senses. That is why optical illusions work, why you never notice the blind spot in each eye, and why you can read "tihs setnence" without trouble.

Lesson 9 · Sleep and your body clock

Two forces decide how sleepy you are. The body clock (a cluster of 20,000 neurons in the hypothalamus called the SCN) is set by daylight reaching the eyes and tells the pineal gland to release melatonin when it gets dark. Sleep pressure is a chemical called adenosine that piles up in the brain the longer you are awake and is cleared only by sleep. Caffeine works by sitting in adenosine's receptors so the brain cannot read the pressure; the adenosine is still there, which is why the crash comes later.

Sleep runs in 90-minute cycles: light sleep, stage 2 (with sleep spindles, when skills are stored), deep slow-wave sleep (memory filing, cleaning), then REM (dreams, emotional processing). Deep sleep dominates the first half of the night, REM the second half. Sleeping 5 hours instead of 8 does not cut each stage a little; it mostly deletes the REM.

What the science says works

  • Bright light within an hour of waking, dim warm light in the evening: this sets the clock.
  • Same wake-up time every day, even weekends. The clock cares more about wake time than bedtime.
  • Caffeine has a half-life of 5–6 hours: a coffee at 16:00 is still a quarter-strength coffee at midnight.
  • Cool, dark room; body temperature must fall about 1 °C to fall asleep.
  • Alcohol makes you fall asleep faster but destroys REM and fragments the second half of the night.

Lesson 10 · The brain across a lifetime

  • Before birth: at peak, 250,000 new neurons are made every minute. Most of the neurons of the cortex exist at birth; the cerebellum keeps adding its tiny granule cells through the first year.
  • Babies and toddlers: synapses explode. A two-year-old has about twice as many synapses as an adult. The brain is deciding which ones to keep.
  • Childhood: "use it or lose it". Connections that are used get myelin and survive; unused ones are pruned. This is why early languages, music and sports feel effortless later.
  • Teenagers: the reward system (dopamine) is fully switched on by about 13, but the prefrontal cortex, which does the braking, keeps maturing until about 25. The gap is biological, not laziness. Teens also have a later body clock, so early school starts fight their biology.
  • Adults: plasticity never stops. London taxi drivers grow the back of their hippocampus memorising the city; musicians enlarge their motor and auditory cortex; learning a language in your 30s still thickens cortex.
  • Older age: processing speed and working memory slow, but vocabulary, knowledge and emotional stability keep improving into the 70s. Exercise, learning, sleep and social contact build "cognitive reserve", which delays the effects of brain ageing by years.

Lesson 11 · Language and the bilingual brain

Language runs on a left-hemisphere network: Wernicke's area (temporal lobe) understands words, Broca's area (frontal lobe) assembles speech, a thick cable called the arcuate fasciculus connects them, the angular gyrus handles reading, and motor cortex drives the tongue and lips. Damage to Wernicke's area gives fluent speech without meaning; damage to Broca's gives meaning without fluency.

Learning a second language reuses the same network. At first the prefrontal cortex works hard to suppress the first language and hold rules in working memory; with practice the new language becomes automatic, handled increasingly by the basal ganglia like any skill. In adults who learned a language intensively, the hippocampus and the language cortex measurably thicken within months.

Why children sound native and adults keep an accent: the sound categories of the first language are carved in during the first year, and the mouth movements become motor habits. Adults can still improve pronunciation a lot, but it needs explicit feedback and deliberate motor practice, like a sport.

Benefits: bilingual people constantly practise choosing between languages, which trains executive control, and on average they show dementia symptoms about four years later than monolinguals with the same disease.

How to use this when you study a language

  • Listen and read a lot of material you mostly understand: the network learns patterns from input, not from rules alone.
  • Speak early and often, even badly: pronunciation is motor learning, which needs repetition and feedback.
  • Learn words inside sentences and situations: episodic hooks (where, with whom, what happened) give the hippocampus more routes to the word.
  • Test yourself with spaced recall, and sleep: both from Lesson 6.
  • Emotion helps: a word you laughed about, or needed in a real conversation, is tagged by dopamine and noradrenaline.

Addiction is the reward system being hijacked. Drugs release far more dopamine than any natural reward, so the brain learns "this matters most", cuts its own dopamine receptors (so normal life feels flat), and prefrontal control weakens. "Wanting" grows while "liking" shrinks. Recovery is possible because the same plasticity that built the habit can rebuild, but it takes months, and cues (places, people) keep their power for years.

How to read a brain headline

  • "Region X lights up during Y" means blood flow rose a few percent; it does not mean X is "the Y centre".
  • Ask: how many people, were they humans or mice, and was it correlation or an intervention?
  • A colourful brain picture is a statistical map, not a photograph.

Lesson 15 · The experiments that built neuroscience

  • 1780s · Galvani makes a frog's leg twitch with electricity: nerves are electrical.
  • 1848 · Phineas Gage survives an iron rod through his frontal lobe; his personality changes. The frontal lobe does self-control.
  • 1861 · Paul Broca finds a patient who understands but cannot speak, with damage in the left frontal lobe.
  • 1906 · Cajal and Golgi share a Nobel Prize: the brain is made of separate cells (neurons), not one continuous net.
  • 1921 · Otto Loewi dreams the experiment that proves neurons communicate with chemicals, using two frog hearts.
  • 1952 · Hodgkin and Huxley explain the spike with sodium and potassium channels in the squid giant axon (Neuron tab).
  • 1953 · Patient H.M. loses both hippocampi in surgery and can never form new memories again; memory has parts.
  • 1959 · Hubel and Wiesel find neurons in the visual cortex that respond only to edges at one angle: the cortex builds vision from features.
  • 1960s · Roger Sperry studies split-brain patients: two hemispheres, two somewhat different minds.
  • 1971 · John O'Keefe discovers place cells in the hippocampus: an inner map. Nobel Prize 2014, with the Mosers' grid cells.
  • 1973 · Bliss and Lømo record LTP for the first time: synapses can strengthen for hours and days.
  • 2000 · Eric Kandel wins the Nobel Prize for showing, in a sea slug, that memory is stored as changes in synapses.
  • 2005 · Optogenetics: neurons switched on with light; cause and effect in living circuits.
  • 2024 · the complete wiring diagram of an adult fruit fly brain, 140,000 neurons and 50 million synapses.

Myths vs facts

MythFact
"We use only 10% of our brain."You use virtually all of it, every day. Brain scans show no silent 90%. A damaged area almost always causes a deficit.
"People are left-brained or right-brained."Some functions lean to one side (language is usually left), but every task uses both halves, connected by the corpus callosum.
"Brain cells cannot grow back."Synapses change all your life, and the hippocampus produces some new neurons even in adults. Learning physically reshapes the brain.
"I can multitask."The brain switches, it does not share. Each switch costs time and errors, and little is remembered.
"Learning styles: I am a visual learner."Decades of tests find no benefit from matching teaching to a claimed style. What works for everyone: attention, meaning, retrieval, spacing, sleep.
"Memory is like a video recording."It is a reconstruction, rebuilt each time from pieces, and it can be edited by the rebuilding.

      5 · Talking points for the teacher

      1. Learning a language physically changes the brain. Adults who studied a language intensively for three months showed measurable growth in the hippocampus and in language cortex (Mårtensson et al., 2012, interpreter trainees). Bilingual adults have denser grey matter in left parietal cortex, more so the earlier and the better they learned (Mechelli et al., 2004).
      2. Two systems work together. The hippocampus records new words fast but fragile; the cortex stores them slowly but permanently. Sleep moves words from the first to the second. A night without sleep after a lesson wastes much of the lesson.
      3. Frequency beats duration. Spacing practice over days produces far better long-term memory than the same time in one block (Cepeda et al., 2006 review of 254 studies). Testing yourself beats re-reading (Dunlosky et al., 2013).
      4. Speaking is a motor skill. Pronunciation lives in motor cortex, basal ganglia and cerebellum, and is learned like a sport: many short repetitions with feedback. "Just listening" is not enough for the mouth.
      5. Sound categories are set early. Babies lose the ability to hear foreign contrasts between 6 and 12 months (Kuhl). Older learners can retrain the auditory cortex, but they need many clear examples with feedback.
      6. Effort is a sign of learning, not failure. The prefrontal cortex works hardest at the start; the feeling of difficulty is the brain building the road. Automatic, effortless speech comes when the basal ganglia take over.
      7. Mistakes are fuel. The cerebellum and the error-monitoring cortex learn from the difference between what you meant and what came out. A class where mistakes are safe learns faster.
      8. Being bilingual trains the executive brain. Switching and suppressing languages exercises prefrontal control; on average bilingual people show dementia symptoms about four years later (Bialystok et al., 2007), though the size of the everyday "bilingual advantage" is still debated.
      9. Emotion and reward tag memories. Words used in a real, funny or meaningful situation get dopamine and noradrenaline tags and survive longer than words from a list.

      The timeline and the simulator are simplified models for teaching: the shapes and directions are faithful to the research, the exact numbers are illustrative.

It is a set of hypotheses to test, not data.

      Reading the model. Giving the full answer maximises immediate performance and confidence and minimises retention: the student recognises instead of generates, so the hippocampus has little to encode and dopamine has no prediction error to tag. A hint after a genuine attempt keeps most of the generation while preventing the unproductive-struggle collapse that appears when the task is far too hard. The gap between confidence and one-week recall is the "illusion of competence" that answer-first learners show.

      Cognitive science that the model relies on

      • Generation effect (Slamecka & Graf 1978) and testing effect (Roediger & Karpicke 2006): what you produce or retrieve is remembered far better than what you read.
      • Desirable difficulties (Bjork): conditions that slow immediate performance often improve long-term learning.
      • Productive failure (Kapur): struggling first, then receiving instruction, beats instruction first, provided consolidation follows.
      • Cognitive load theory (Sweller): worked examples and subgoal labels (Margulieux) cut extraneous load for novices; faded support keeps germane load.
      • Parsons problems (Ericson, Denny) and tracing before writing (Lister; Lopez et al. 2008; Xie et al. 2019): constrained generation that builds schemas with manageable load.
      • Expertise as chunking (Chase & Simon; Soloway & Ehrlich 1984): experts perceive plans and patterns where novices see lines.

      5 · From model to study: testable hypotheses and measures

      Hypotheses for "the cost of easy answers"

      • H1 (generation): students who attempt an exercise before seeing an AI-generated solution retain more one week later than students who see the solution first, at equal or lower immediate performance.
      • H2 (calibration): solution-first students report higher confidence relative to their delayed-test score (larger confidence–performance gap).
      • H3 (engagement): physiological engagement during the attempt (EEG engagement index β/(α+θ), pupil dilation, prefrontal fNIRS) is higher in attempt-first conditions and predicts delayed retention.
      • H4 (transfer): attempt-first students solve near-transfer problems (same pattern, new surface) better; the advantage shrinks on identical problems.
      • H5 (dose): hints delivered after a genuine attempt preserve most of the retention advantage while reducing time and frustration; full solutions before the attempt do not.

      A minimal design

      Within-subject, counterbalanced: each student meets matched exercises in three conditions (attempt-then-hint, attempt-then-full-solution, solution-first). Immediate test, confidence rating, one-week delayed test with transfer items. Log keystrokes and AI interactions; add an eye tracker or EEG headset for a subsample. Analyse retention and calibration with mixed models (student and exercise as random effects). Pre-register the hypotheses above.
